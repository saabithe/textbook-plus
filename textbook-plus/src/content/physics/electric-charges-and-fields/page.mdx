# Electric Charges and Fields

---

## 1.1 Electric Charge

* All of us have the experience of seeing a spark or hearing a crackle when we take off our synthetic clothes or sweater, particularly in dry weather.
* Another common example of electric discharge is the lightning that we see in the sky during thunderstorms.
* We also experience a sensation of an electric shock either while opening the door of a car or holding the iron bar of a bus after sliding from our seat.
* The reason for these experiences is discharge of electric charges through our body, which were accumulated due to rubbing of insulating surfaces.

<Callout type="important">
**Electrostatics** deals with the study of forces, fields and potentials arising from static charges. Static means anything that does not move or change with time.
</Callout>

* Historically, the credit of discovery of the fact that amber rubbed with wool or silk cloth attracts light objects goes to Thales of Miletus, Greece, around 600 BC.
* The name electricity is coined from the Greek word *elektron* meaning amber.
* It was observed that if two glass rods rubbed with wool or silk cloth are brought close to each other, they repel each other.
* The two strands of wool or two pieces of silk cloth, with which the rods were rubbed, also repel each other.
* However, the glass rod and wool attracted each other.
* Similarly, two plastic rods rubbed with cat's fur repelled each other but attracted the fur.

<Callout type="important">
There are two kinds of electrification: **(i) like charges repel** and **(ii) unlike charges attract** each other. The property which differentiates the two kinds of charges is called the **polarity of charge**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="important">
The charges were named as **positive** and **negative** by Benjamin Franklin. By convention, the charge on glass rod rubbed with silk is **positive**; that on plastic rod rubbed with fur is **negative**.
</Callout>

* A simple apparatus to detect charge on a body is the **gold-leaf electroscope**.
* It consists of a vertical metal rod housed in a box, with two thin gold leaves attached to its bottom end.
* When a charged object touches the metal knob at the top, charge flows to the leaves and they diverge.
* The degree of divergence indicates the amount of charge.
* To electrify a neutral body, we need to add or remove one kind of charge.
* In solids, some electrons, being less tightly bound, are transferred from one body to another.
* A body is charged positively by losing electrons, negatively by gaining electrons.
* When we rub a glass rod with silk, some electrons transfer from rod to silk — the rod becomes positive, silk becomes negative.
* No new charge is created; only transferred.

## 1.2 Conductors and Insulators

* **Conductors**: charges flow freely through them
  * Metals (copper, aluminium, silver), human and animal bodies, earth
  * In metals: mobile charges are **electrons**
  * In electrolytes: both **positive and negative ions** are mobile
* **Insulators**: charges do not flow
  * Glass, rubber, plastic, wood, porcelain, nylon
* **Semiconductors**: intermediate conductivity
  * Silicon, germanium
  * Conductivity can be controlled (basis of electronics)

* When some charge is transferred to a conductor, it readily gets distributed over the entire surface.
* In contrast, if some charge is put on an insulator, it stays at the same place.
* This explains why a nylon or plastic comb gets electrified on combing dry hair, but a metal article like spoon does not — the charges on metal leak through our body to the ground as both are conductors.

## 1.3 Charging by Induction

**Process — step by step:**

1. Bring a charged body (e.g., positive rod) near a neutral conductor
2. Free electrons in conductor move toward the rod (attracted)
3. Near side of conductor becomes **negative**, far side becomes **positive**
4. If conductor is **grounded**, far-side charge flows to earth
5. Remove ground first, then remove the inducing body
6. Conductor retains the opposite charge — **permanently charged**

<Callout type="important">
In induction, no charge is transferred by contact. The charged body induces redistribution of charges in the neutral conductor.
</Callout>

## 1.4 Basic Properties of Electric Charges

* If the sizes of charged bodies are very small compared to the distances between them, we treat them as **point charges** — all charge concentrated at one point.

### Quantisation

* Charge exists in discrete packets, not continuous

<Formula>$$q = ne$$</Formula>

* $n$ = integer ($0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \ldots$)
* $e = 1.602192 \times 10^{-19}$ C (magnitude of electron/proton charge)
* First suggested by Faraday's laws of electrolysis, demonstrated by Millikan in 1912
* For macroscopic objects, $n$ is enormous → quantisation can be ignored

<Callout type="note">
At the macroscopic level (e.g., $1 \mu\text{C}$ contains $\sim 10^{13}$ electronic charges), quantisation has no practical consequence. At the microscopic level (tens or hundreds of $e$), quantisation cannot be ignored.
</Callout>

### Additivity

* Total charge = algebraic sum of all individual charges

<Formula>$$q_{\text{total}} = q_1 + q_2 + q_3 + \ldots$$</Formula>

* Signs matter: $+5\mu\text{C} + (-3\mu\text{C}) = +2\mu\text{C}$
* Charge has magnitude but no direction, similar to mass — but mass is always positive while charge can be positive or negative

### Conservation

* Total charge of an **isolated system** is constant
* Charge can transfer between bodies but is never created or destroyed
* Rubbing two bodies: charges appear on both, but net charge stays zero
* Sometimes nature creates charged particles: a neutron turns into a proton and an electron — total charge remains zero

<Callout type="important">
These three — quantisation, additivity, conservation — are the fundamental properties of electric charge.
</Callout>

## 1.5 Coulomb's Law

<Callout type="important">
Coulomb measured the force between two point charges and found that it varied inversely as the square of the distance between the charges and was directly proportional to the product of the magnitude of the two charges and acted along the line joining the two charges.
</Callout>

<Formula title="Coulomb's Law (scalar form)">
$$F = k \frac{|q_1 q_2|}{r^2}$$
</Formula>

<Formula title="Coulomb's Law (vector form)">
$$\vec{F}_{21} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{21}^2} \hat{r}_{21}$$
</Formula>

* $\hat{r}_{21}$ = unit vector from $q_1$ to $q_2$
* $k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \approx 9 \times 10^9$ N·m²/C²
* $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12}$ C²/(N·m²) — permittivity of free space

<Expandable title="How Coulomb discovered his law">
Coulomb used a **torsion balance** to measure force between charged metallic spheres. The charges were unknown initially. His clever method: put a charged sphere in contact with an identical uncharged sphere — by symmetry, each gets $q/2$. Repeat to get $q/4$, etc. He varied distance for fixed charges, then varied charges for fixed distance, and compared forces to arrive at the inverse-square law.
</Expandable>

<Expandable title="Remarks on the vector form">
* Valid for any sign of $q_1$ and $q_2$: same sign → repulsion (along $\hat{r}_{21}$), opposite sign → attraction (along $-\hat{r}_{21}$)
* Force $\vec{F}_{12}$ on $q_1$ due to $q_2$ is obtained by swapping indices: $\vec{F}_{12} = -\vec{F}_{21}$ — agrees with Newton's third law
* The law gives force in vacuum. In matter, the situation is more complicated (next chapter)
</Expandable>

<KeyPoint title="Remember">
Coulomb's Law: $F = k|q_1q_2|/r^2$. Like gravity, it's an inverse-square law — but unlike gravity, it can be attractive **or** repulsive.
</KeyPoint>

<Callout type="didyouknow">
The ratio of electric force to gravitational force between a proton and an electron is approximately $10^{39}$. Electric force is vastly stronger.
</Callout>

## 1.6 Forces between Multiple Charges

<Callout type="important">
**Superposition Principle:** The force on any charge = vector sum of forces due to all other charges individually. Each pair follows Coulomb's Law independently.
</Callout>

* No "three-body" or "four-body" forces exist
* Experimentally verified: individual forces are unaffected by presence of other charges

<Formula title="Superposition">
$$\vec{F}_1 = \vec{F}_{12} + \vec{F}_{13} + \ldots + \vec{F}_{1n} = \sum_{i=2}^{n} \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_i}{r_{1i}^2} \hat{r}_{1i}$$
</Formula>

* All of electrostatics is a consequence of Coulomb's law and the superposition principle.

## 1.7 Electric Field

* Action at a distance is hard to conceptualise. We introduce an intermediary — the field.
* A charge creates a field around it. Another charge placed in that field experiences a force.
* If charge $q$ is removed from point $P$, what is left? The early scientists introduced the concept of **field**: charge $Q$ produces an electric field everywhere. When $q$ is brought to $P$, the field acts on it.
* Electric field at a point = force per unit positive test charge.

<Formula title="Electric Field due to a Point Charge">
$$\vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r^2} \hat{r}$$
</Formula>

<Formula title="Force on a Charge in an Electric Field">
$$\vec{F} = q\vec{E}$$
</Formula>

* Direction: radially outward ($Q > 0$), radially inward ($Q < 0$)
* SI unit: N/C or V/m
* Field due to a system of charges: apply superposition to individual fields

<Expandable title="Important remarks about electric field">
(i) The **source charge** $Q$ must remain at its original location. The **test charge** $q$ should be negligibly small so it doesn't disturb $Q$. The field is defined as $\vec{E} = \lim_{q \to 0}(\vec{F}/q)$.

(ii) $\vec{E}$ is independent of the test charge $q$ — it depends only on the source charge and position.

(iii) For positive source charge, $\vec{E}$ points radially outward. For negative, radially inward.

(iv) Magnitude of $\vec{E}$ depends only on distance $r$ — has spherical symmetry.
</Expandable>

<Callout type="note">
Electric field is a **vector field** — it has magnitude and direction at every point in space. It satisfies the superposition principle.
</Callout>

### 1.7.1 Physical significance of electric field

* For electrostatics, the field concept is convenient but not really necessary — we could use Coulomb's law directly.
* The true significance emerges beyond electrostatics: when charges are in accelerated motion, the effect cannot arise instantaneously (limited by speed of light $c$).
* The field picture handles this naturally — accelerated charge produces electromagnetic waves that propagate at $c$ and cause force on distant charges.
* The concept of field was first introduced by Faraday and is now among the central concepts in physics.
* Fields are physical entities, not merely mathematical constructs — they have independent dynamics and can transport energy.

## 1.8 Electric Field Lines

* Imaginary curves where the tangent at any point gives the direction of $\vec{E}$
* Invented by Faraday (called them "lines of force")
* **Density** of field lines = field strength (crowded near strong field, sparse near weak)
* In uniform field: parallel, equally spaced straight lines

**Figure 1.12:** Field of a point charge — lines radiate outward, density decreases as $1/r^2$.

**Figure 1.14:** Field lines around simple charge configurations: single positive (radially outward), single negative (radially inward), two positives (repulsion pattern), dipole (attraction pattern).

<Expandable title="Why field line density represents field strength">
Consider a solid angle $\Delta\Omega$ from a point charge. The number of radial field lines in this solid angle is the same at all distances. At distance $r_1$, the area subtending $\Delta\Omega$ is $r_1^2\Delta\Omega$. At $r_2$, it's $r_2^2\Delta\Omega$. Same number of lines cutting these areas means field line density $\propto 1/r^2$ — matching the field's $1/r^2$ dependence.
</Expandable>

**Properties:**

| # | Property | Why |
|---|----------|-----|
| 1 | Continuous curves, no breaks | Field exists at every point in charge-free regions |
| 2 | Two field lines **never cross** | Field at a point has unique direction — crossing implies two directions |
| 3 | Start from positive charges, end at negative charges | By convention (direction of force on positive test charge) |
| 4 | Closeness = field strength | More lines per unit area = stronger field |
| 5 | Cannot form closed loops in electrostatics | Follows from conservative nature of electric field |

<Callout type="warning">
If field lines crossed, the field at the intersection would have two different directions simultaneously — physically impossible.
</Callout>

## 1.9 Electric Flux

* Flux measures how much "field" passes through a surface.
* Think of rain falling on a window. More rain passes through when the window faces the rain directly ($\theta = 0^\circ$) than when it's tilted.

<Expandable title="Liquid flow analogy">
Consider liquid flowing with velocity $\vec{v}$ through a small flat surface $dS$ normal to the flow. Volume crossing per unit time = $v \, dS$ — this is the flux of liquid. If the surface is tilted at angle $\theta$ to the flow, the projected area is $dS\cos\theta$, so flux = $v \, dS \cos\theta$. Electric flux is analogous — but there's no actual flow of physical quantity.
</Expandable>

### Area vector concept

* An area element should be treated as a **vector** — it has magnitude and direction.
* The direction of a planar area vector is along its **normal**.
* For a closed surface, the convention is: vector associated with every area element points in the direction of the **outward normal**.

<Formula title="Electric Flux">
$$\Delta\Phi = \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{S} = E \, dS \cos\theta$$
</Formula>

* $\theta$ = angle between $\vec{E}$ and the outward normal to the surface
* SI unit: V·m or N·m²/C
* $\theta = 0^\circ$: flux maximum (surface ⊥ field)
* $\theta = 90^\circ$: flux zero (surface ∥ field)

<Expandable title="Derivation — flux through a sphere enclosing a point charge">
For a point charge $q$ at the centre of a sphere of radius $r$:

* Field at every point on sphere: $E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}$ (radial)
* Area element $dA$ is also radial → $\vec{E} \parallel d\vec{A}$ everywhere
* Total flux: $\Phi = E \times 4\pi r^2 = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2} \times 4\pi r^2 = \frac{q}{\varepsilon_0}$

This is Gauss's Law — the flux depends only on enclosed charge, not on the radius.
</Expandable>

## 1.10 Electric Dipole

* Most charge distributions have net zero charge but still produce fields (e.g., molecules). The dipole is the simplest such system.
* Two equal and opposite charges don't cancel completely at finite distances — their fields partially survive, creating a distinctive $1/r^3$ pattern.

### Definition

* Pair of equal and opposite charges $+q$ and $-q$, separated by distance $2a$
* The total charge is zero, but the fields don't exactly cancel at finite distances
* At $r \gg 2a$, the fields nearly cancel → field falls faster than $1/r^2$

<Formula title="Dipole Moment">
$$\vec{p} = q \times 2a\,\hat{p}$$
</Formula>

* Direction: from $-q$ to $+q$
* SI unit: C·m

### Field on the axis ($r \gg a$)

<Formula>
$$\vec{E}_{\text{axis}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{2\vec{p}}{r^3}$$
</Formula>

### Field on the equatorial plane ($r \gg a$)

<Formula>
$$\vec{E}_{\text{equatorial}} = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{\vec{p}}{r^3}$$
</Formula>

<Callout type="important">
Dipole field falls as $1/r^3$, not $1/r^2$. This is because the two charges partially cancel at large distances. The magnitude and direction depend not only on $r$ but also on the angle between $\vec{r}$ and $\vec{p}$.
</Callout>

### Exact expressions (not limited to $r \gg a$)

<Expandable title="Full derivation — dipole field on axis">
Point $P$ at distance $r$ from centre, on the side of $+q$:

* Field due to $+q$: $E_+ = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0(r-a)^2}$ (away from dipole)
* Field due to $-q$: $E_- = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0(r+a)^2}$ (toward dipole)
* Net field: $E = E_+ - E_- = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{1}{(r-a)^2} - \frac{1}{(r+a)^2}\right]$
* Simplify: $E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{4ar}{(r^2-a^2)^2}$
* For $r \gg a$: $E \approx \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{2p}{r^3}$
</Expandable>

<Expandable title="Full derivation — dipole field on equatorial plane">
Point $Q$ at distance $r$ from centre, on the perpendicular bisector:

* Magnitudes: $E_+ = E_- = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0(r^2+a^2)}$
* Components perpendicular to dipole axis cancel
* Components along dipole axis add: $E = 2E_+\cos\theta = 2E_+\frac{a}{\sqrt{r^2+a^2}}$
* For $r \gg a$: $E \approx \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{p}{r^3}$ (opposite to $\vec{p}$)
</Expandable>

### Physical significance of dipoles

* Most molecules: centres of + and − charges coincide → zero dipole moment
* **Polar molecules** (H₂O, HCl): permanent dipole moment — centres of positive and negative charges do not coincide
* **Non-polar molecules** (CO₂, CH₄): zero dipole moment normally, but develop induced dipole moment in external field

### Point dipole

* Idealised limit: $2a \to 0$, $q \to \infty$, but $p = q \times 2a$ is finite
* For point dipole, $1/r^3$ expressions are exact for any $r$

## 1.11 Dipole in a Uniform External Field

* Net force on dipole in **uniform** field = **zero** (equal and opposite forces cancel)
* But forces act at different points → **torque**

<Formula title="Torque on a Dipole">
$$\vec{\tau} = \vec{p} \times \vec{E}$$
</Formula>

* Magnitude: $\tau = pE\sin\theta$
* $\theta = 0^\circ$: $\vec{p} \parallel \vec{E}$, torque = 0 (stable equilibrium)
* $\theta = 180^\circ$: $\vec{p}$ antiparallel to $\vec{E}$, torque = 0 (unstable equilibrium)

<Expandable title="Derivation — torque on a dipole">
* Force on $+q$: $\vec{F}_+ = q\vec{E}$ (along $\vec{E}$)
* Force on $-q$: $\vec{F}_- = -q\vec{E}$ (opposite to $\vec{E}$)
* Net force: $\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = q\vec{E} - q\vec{E} = 0$
* Torque about centre: each force contributes $qE \times a\sin\theta$
* Total torque: $\tau = 2qaE\sin\theta = pE\sin\theta$
* Vector form: $\vec{\tau} = \vec{p} \times \vec{E}$
</Expandable>

<Expandable title="Non-uniform field — net force on dipole">
In a **non-uniform** field, the dipole experiences a **net force** in addition to torque. When $\vec{p} \parallel \vec{E}$, force is in direction of increasing field. When $\vec{p}$ is antiparallel to $\vec{E}$, force is in direction of decreasing field.

**Common example:** A charged comb attracts neutral paper. The comb polarises the paper (induces dipole moment). The non-uniform field of the comb exerts a net force on the induced dipole, attracting the paper.
</Expandable>

## 1.12 Continuous Charge Distribution

* For large distributions, it's impractical to work with discrete charges. We use **charge density** instead.

| Type | Symbol | Definition | Unit |
|------|--------|------------|------|
| Linear | $\lambda$ | $\Delta Q / \Delta l$ | C/m |
| Surface | $\sigma$ | $\Delta Q / \Delta S$ | C/m² |
| Volume | $\rho$ | $\Delta Q / \Delta V$ | C/m³ |

<Callout type="note">
The notion is similar to continuous mass distribution in mechanics. We regard a liquid as a continuous fluid and ignore discrete molecular constitution — same idea applies to charge density.
</Callout>

* Field from continuous distribution:

<Formula>
$$\vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \int \frac{\rho \, dV}{r'^2} \hat{r}'$$
</Formula>

* In practice, choose a Gaussian surface with symmetry to avoid integration

## 1.13 Gauss's Law

<Callout type="important">
The total electric flux through any closed surface is equal to $\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}$ times the net charge enclosed by the surface:

$$\oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = \frac{q}{\varepsilon_0}$$
</Callout>

* $q$ = total charge **enclosed** by the surface $S$
* The surface $S$ is called the **Gaussian surface**

### Key points

1. True for **any** closed surface, regardless of shape or size
2. $q$ = sum of all charges **inside** the surface
3. $\vec{E}$ on the left side is due to **all** charges (inside + outside); $q$ on the right is only enclosed charges
4. Gaussian surface must not pass through **discrete charges** (field undefined at point charges)
5. Useful for calculating $\vec{E}$ when charge distribution has **symmetry**
6. Based on the inverse-square law — any violation of Gauss's Law indicates departure from $1/r^2$

<Expandable title="Zero flux implies zero enclosed charge">
Consider a uniform electric field through a closed cylindrical surface with axis parallel to $\vec{E}$. Flux through the curved surface = 0 (field ⊥ normal). Flux through the two flat faces: $\Phi_1 = -ES$, $\Phi_2 = +ES$. Total flux = 0. By Gauss's law, total enclosed charge = 0. This confirms: zero net flux → zero net enclosed charge.
</Expandable>

<KeyPoint title="Key exam point">
Gauss's Law: $\oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = q/\varepsilon_0$. The power is in choosing the right Gaussian surface — symmetry is everything.
</KeyPoint>

<Callout type="important">
Gauss's Law is equivalent to Coulomb's Law but far more powerful for symmetric charge distributions. The choice of Gaussian surface is critical.
</Callout>

## 1.14 Applications of Gauss's Law

### Infinite straight wire (linear charge density $\lambda$)

<Expandable title="Derivation — field of an infinite wire">
* **Symmetry:** field is radial, depends only on $r$ (wire is axis of symmetry)
* Pair of line elements produce resultant radial field (normal components cancel)
* Field doesn't depend on position along wire (infinite wire)
* **Gaussian surface:** coaxial cylinder of radius $r$, length $l$
* Flux through two ends = 0 (field ⊥ to end normals)
* Flux through curved surface: $E \times 2\pi rl$
* Enclosed charge: $\lambda l$
* Gauss's Law: $E \times 2\pi rl = \frac{\lambda l}{\varepsilon_0}$
</Expandable>

<Formula title="Field of an Infinite Wire">
$$E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$$
</Formula>

* Direction: radial (outward if $\lambda > 0$, inward if $\lambda < 0$)
* Depends on $r$ (not on position along wire)
* Approximately true for central portions of a long wire (end effects ignored)

### Infinite plane sheet (surface charge density $\sigma$)

<Expandable title="Derivation — field of an infinite plane sheet">
* **Symmetry:** field is perpendicular to sheet, same on both sides (doesn't depend on $y, z$)
* **Gaussian surface:** rectangular parallelepiped with faces parallel to sheet
* Flux through sides = 0 (field ∥ to side normals)
* Flux through two faces: $2EA$
* Enclosed charge: $\sigma A$
* Gauss's Law: $2EA = \frac{\sigma A}{\varepsilon_0}$
</Expandable>

<Formula title="Field of an Infinite Plane Sheet">
$$E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}$$
</Formula>

* Direction: perpendicular to sheet, outward on either side
* **Independent of distance** from the sheet ($E$ doesn't depend on $x$)

### Thin spherical shell (radius $R$, total charge $q$)

<Expandable title="Derivation — field of a spherical shell">
**Outside ($r \geq R$):**
* Spherical symmetry → field is radial, depends only on $r$
* Gaussian surface: sphere of radius $r$ concentric with shell
* By symmetry, $E$ is constant and radial on this surface
* Flux: $E \times 4\pi r^2$
* Enclosed charge: $q = 4\pi R^2\sigma$
* Gauss's Law: $E \times 4\pi r^2 = \frac{q}{\varepsilon_0}$

**Inside ($r < R$):**
* Same Gaussian surface, but it encloses **no charge**
* $E \times 4\pi r^2 = 0$ → $E = 0$
</Expandable>

<Formula title="Field of a Spherical Shell">
$$E = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2} & r \geq R \\ 0 & r < R \end{cases}$$
</Formula>

* Outside: field as if all charge concentrated at centre
* Inside: field = **zero**
* Same result applies to solid sphere of uniform volume charge density
* Experimental verification of this result confirms the $1/r^2$ dependence in Coulomb's law

<Callout type="important">
The field inside a uniformly charged spherical shell is zero. This is a direct consequence of Gauss's Law which follows from Coulomb's Law.
</Callout>

## Examples

<Example title="Example 1.1 — Time to Accumulate 1 Coulomb">
If $10^9$ electrons move out of a body every second, how much time is required to get 1 C on the other body?

Charge per second: $1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 10^9 = 1.6 \times 10^{-10}$ C/s

Time = $1 \text{ C} \div (1.6 \times 10^{-10} \text{ C/s}) = 6.25 \times 10^9$ s $\approx 198$ years

<Callout type="note">
One coulomb is a very large unit. A 1 cm³ piece of copper contains about $2.5 \times 10^{24}$ electrons.
</Callout>
</Example>

<Example title="Example 1.2 — Charge in a Cup of Water">
How much positive and negative charge in 250 g of water?

Molecules = $(250/18) \times 6.02 \times 10^{23}$. Each molecule has 10 electrons + 10 protons.

Total charge = $(250/18) \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} \times 10 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 1.34 \times 10^7$ C
</Example>

<Example title="Example 1.3 — Ratio of Electric to Gravitational Force">
(a) Electron-proton: $F_e/F_G = e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 G m_p m_e) = 2.4 \times 10^{39}$

Two protons: $F_e/F_G = 1.3 \times 10^{36}$

(b) At 1 Å apart: $|F| = 2.3 \times 10^{-8}$ N. Electron acceleration: $2.5 \times 10^{22}$ m/s². Proton acceleration: $1.4 \times 10^{19}$ m/s².

<Callout type="note">
Electrical forces are enormously stronger than gravitational forces. Gravitational effects are negligible at atomic scales.
</Callout>
</Example>

<Example title="Example 1.4 — Coulomb's Law with Charge Sharing">
Charged spheres A and B repel at 10 cm. Touch each with uncharged spheres C and D (identical sizes). Halve the distance to 5 cm.

After touching: A has $q/2$, B has $q'/2$. Force $F' = \frac{(q/2)(q'/2)}{(r/2)^2} \cdot \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = \frac{qq'}{r^2} \cdot \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = F$

The force remains **unaltered** — charge halved but distance also halved.
</Example>

<Example title="Example 1.5 — Force at Centroid of Equilateral Triangle">
Three charges $q$ at vertices of equilateral triangle (side $l$). Force on charge $Q$ at centroid?

By symmetry, forces from all three vertices sum to zero. If the resultant were non-zero in some direction, rotating the system by 60° about O would change that direction — but the system is unchanged. Contradiction. So $\vec{F}_{\text{net}} = 0$.
</Example>

<Example title="Example 1.6 — Mixed Charges at Triangle Vertices">
Charges $q, q, -q$ at vertices. Force on each charge?

Each pair has force magnitude $F = q^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 l^2)$. By parallelogram law:
* Force on $q$ at A: $\vec{F}_1 = F\hat{r}_1$ (along BC)
* Force on $q$ at B: $\vec{F}_2 = F\hat{r}_2$ (along AC)
* Force on $-q$ at C: $\vec{F}_3 = \sqrt{3}F\hat{n}$

Sum: $\vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2 + \vec{F}_3 = 0$ (consistent with Newton's third law).
</Example>

<Example title="Example 1.7 — Electron and Proton Falling in Electric Field">
An electron falls through $h = 1.5$ cm in a uniform electric field $E = 2.0 \times 10^4$ N/C. The field direction is reversed and a proton falls through the same distance. Compute the time of fall in each case.

**Electron:**
* Downward force: $F = eE$ (field is upward, electron is negative)
* Acceleration: $a_e = eE/m_e$
* Time: $t_e = \sqrt{2h/a_e} = \sqrt{2hm_e/(eE)} = 2.9 \times 10^{-9}$ s

**Proton:**
* Downward force: $F = eE$ (field is downward, proton is positive)
* Acceleration: $a_p = eE/m_p$
* Time: $t_p = \sqrt{2hm_p/(eE)} = 1.3 \times 10^{-7}$ s

<Callout type="note">
The heavier particle (proton) takes greater time — in contrast to free fall under gravity where time is independent of mass. The acceleration due to gravity ($g = 9.8$ m/s²) can be ignored here because $a_p = 1.9 \times 10^{12}$ m/s² $\gg g$.
</Callout>
</Example>

<Example title="Example 1.8 — Electric Field at Three Points">
Two point charges $q_1 = +10^{-8}$ C and $q_2 = -10^{-8}$ C are placed $0.1$ m apart. Calculate the electric field at points A, B, and C.

* **Point A** (midpoint between charges): Both $E_1$ and $E_2$ point right → $E_A = 7.2 \times 10^4$ N/C (right)
* **Point B** (0.15 m from $q_2$): $E_1$ left, $E_2$ right → $E_B = 3.2 \times 10^4$ N/C (left)
* **Point C** (on perpendicular bisector): Both fields at $60^\circ$ → $E_C = 9 \times 10^3$ N/C (right)
</Example>

<Example title="Example 1.9 — Dipole Field on Axis and Equatorial Plane">
Charges $\pm 10\,\mu$C placed $5.0$ mm apart. Find $E$ at (a) point P on axis, 15 cm from centre, and (b) point Q on equatorial plane, 15 cm from centre.

**(a) On axis:**
* $p = q \times 2a = 10^{-5} \times 5 \times 10^{-3} = 5 \times 10^{-8}$ C·m
* Exact: calculate fields from each charge separately, subtract → $2.7 \times 10^5$ N/C
* Approximate ($r >> a$): $E = \frac{2p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^3} = 2.6 \times 10^5$ N/C (along dipole moment)

**(b) On equatorial plane:**
* Exact: calculate fields from each charge, add components → $1.33 \times 10^5$ N/C
* Approximate: $E = \frac{p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^3} = 1.33 \times 10^5$ N/C (opposite to dipole moment)

<Callout type="note">
The dipole field falls as $1/r^3$, not $1/r^2$. The equatorial field is half the axial field at the same distance.
</Callout>
</Example>

<Example title="Example 1.10 — Electric Flux through a Cube (Non-Uniform Field)">
$E_x = \alpha x^{1/2}$, $\alpha = 800$ N/C·m$^{1/2}$. Cube of side $a = 0.1$ m.

* Left face ($x = a$): $E_L = \alpha a^{1/2}$, flux = $-E_L a^2$
* Right face ($x = 2a$): $E_R = \alpha(2a)^{1/2}$, flux = $+E_R a^2$
* Net flux: $\Phi = \alpha a^{5/2}(\sqrt{2} - 1) = 1.05$ N·m²/C
* Charge inside: $q = \varepsilon_0 \Phi = 9.27 \times 10^{-12}$ C
</Example>

<Example title="Example 1.11 — Flux through a Cylinder in Uniform Field">
$E = 200\hat{i}$ N/C for $x > 0$, $E = -200\hat{i}$ for $x < 0$. Cylinder: length 20 cm, radius 5 cm, centred at origin.

* Each flat face: $\Phi = 200 \times \pi(0.05)^2 = 1.57$ N·m²/C
* Side: $\Phi = 0$ (field ⊥ to surface)
* Net flux: $\Phi = 3.14$ N·m²/C
* Charge inside: $q = 2.78 \times 10^{-11}$ C
</Example>

<Example title="Example 1.12 — Atom Model (Gauss's Law Application)">
Positive nucleus ($Ze$) at centre, uniform negative charge density $-Ze$ up to radius $R$.

**Inside ($r < R$):** Enclosed charge = $Ze(1 - r^3/R^3)$

$$E(r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Ze}{r^2}\left(1 - \frac{r^3}{R^3}\right)$$

**Outside ($r > R$):** $E = 0$ (atom is neutral, total enclosed charge = 0)

At $r = R$: both cases give $E = 0$.
</Example>

<Example title="Supplementary Example — Torque on a Dipole">
Dipole moment $p = 10^{-29}$ C·m in field $E = 10^5$ N/C at angle $30^\circ$.

$$\tau = pE\sin\theta = 10^{-29} \times 10^5 \times \sin 30^\circ = 5 \times 10^{-25} \text{ N·m}$$

At $\theta = 90^\circ$ (maximum): $\tau = 10^{-24}$ N·m. At $\theta = 0^\circ$ or $180^\circ$: $\tau = 0$ (equilibrium).
</Example>

<Example title="Supplementary Example — Force on Dipole in Non-Uniform Field">
Dipole $p = 10^{-29}$ C·m in field $E = (10^5 + 10^7 x)$ N/C.

$$F = p \frac{dE}{dx} = 10^{-29} \times 10^7 = 10^{-22} \text{ N}$$

The dipole accelerates toward the stronger-field region.
</Example>

<Example title="Supplementary Example — Infinite Line Charge (Gauss's Law)">
Line charge density $\lambda = 5 \times 10^{-7}$ C/m. Find $E$ at $r = 0.1$ m.

$$E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r} = \frac{5 \times 10^{-7}}{2\pi \times 8.854 \times 10^{-12} \times 0.1} = 8.99 \times 10^4 \text{ N/C}$$
</Example>

<Example title="Supplementary Example — Uniformly Charged Spherical Shell">
Shell: radius $R = 0.1$ m, total charge $q = 10^{-8}$ C.

**Outside ($r = 0.2$ m):**

$$E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2} = \frac{10^{-8} \times 9 \times 10^9}{(0.2)^2} = 2250 \text{ N/C}$$

**Inside ($r = 0.05$ m):** $E = 0$.
</Example>

## Common Mistakes

<KeyPoint title="Dipole moment direction">
Dipole moment is directed from **−q to +q** (not +q to −q). This is a common exam error.
</KeyPoint>

<KeyPoint title="Gauss's Law — E on the left vs q on the right">
The $\vec{E}$ in Gauss's Law is due to **all** charges (inside + outside). But $q$ on the right side is **only** the enclosed charge. Students often confuse these.
</KeyPoint>

<KeyPoint title="Flux through a closed surface with no enclosed charge">
If no charge is enclosed, $\Phi = 0$. This does **not** mean $\vec{E} = 0$ everywhere on the surface — only that the net flux is zero (field lines entering = field lines leaving).
</KeyPoint>

<KeyPoint title="Induction vs conduction">
In induction, no charge is transferred by contact. The charged body only causes redistribution. In conduction, charge physically flows from one body to another.
</KeyPoint>

<KeyPoint title="Superposition is vector addition">
Forces are vectors. Always resolve into components before adding. Common mistake: adding magnitudes directly when forces are at angles.
</KeyPoint>

<KeyPoint title="Field inside a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium">
$E = 0$ inside a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium. If $E$ were nonzero, charges would move — contradicting equilibrium.
</KeyPoint>

## Summary

1. Electric and magnetic forces determine the properties of atoms, molecules and bulk matter.
2. Two types of charges: positive and negative. Like charges repel, unlike attract.
3. Conductors allow charge flow; insulators do not. In metals, mobile charges are electrons; in electrolytes, both positive and negative ions are mobile.
4. Three properties: quantisation ($q = ne$), additivity (algebraic sum), conservation (isolated system charge is constant).
5. Coulomb's Law: $F = k|q_1q_2|/r^2$, $k \approx 9 \times 10^9$ N·m²/C². $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12}$ C²/(N·m²).
6. Ratio of electric to gravitational force between proton and electron: $ke^2/(Gm_pm_e) \approx 10^{39}$.
7. Superposition Principle: force on any charge = vector sum of individual forces. Each pair follows Coulomb's Law independently.
8. Electric field: $\vec{E} = \vec{F}/q$. Due to point charge: $E = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)$. Satisfies superposition.
9. Field lines: continuous, never cross, start from +, end at −. Density = field strength. Cannot form closed loops in electrostatics.
10. Electric flux: $\Phi = EA\cos\theta$. Unit: V·m or N·m²/C.
11. Dipole moment: $p = q \times 2a$. Field falls as $1/r^3$ (vs $1/r^2$ for point charge).
12. Torque on dipole: $\vec{\tau} = \vec{p} \times \vec{E}$. Net force = 0 in uniform field.
13. Continuous distributions: $\lambda = \Delta Q/\Delta l$, $\sigma = \Delta Q/\Delta S$, $\rho = \Delta Q/\Delta V$.
14. Gauss's Law: $\oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{A} = q/\varepsilon_0$. Based on inverse-square law.
15. Applications: infinite wire ($E = \lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0 r$), plane sheet ($E = \sigma/2\varepsilon_0$), spherical shell ($E = 0$ inside, $q/4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2$ outside).

## Points to Ponder

1. Protons in the nucleus are held together by the **strong nuclear force** (range $\sim 10^{-14}$ m) — much stronger than electromagnetic repulsion. Electrons are kept outside the nucleus by quantum mechanics.
2. Gravity is always attractive, but Coulomb force can be attractive or repulsive — allowing cancellation. This is why gravity dominates at large scales despite being weaker.
3. The constant $k$ in Coulomb's law is arbitrary if charge is defined via Coulomb's law. In SI units, current (A) is defined via magnetic effects, so $k \approx 9 \times 10^9$ N·m²/C² is fixed.
4. The large value of $k$ (large unit of charge 1C) arises because charge is defined via magnetic forces, which are generally much weaker than electric forces.
5. Additivity of charge is not obvious — it's related to the fact that charge is a scalar (no direction).
6. Charge is not only a scalar under rotation but also **invariant under frame change** — unlike kinetic energy.
7. Conservation of charge is independent of the scalar nature of charge. A quantity may be scalar but not conserved (kinetic energy in inelastic collision). One can have conserved vector quantities (angular momentum).
8. Quantisation of charge is a fundamental law of nature; there is no analogous quantisation of mass.
9. Superposition is not just vector addition — it says forces between pairs are unaffected by other charges, and no three-body forces exist.
10. Electric field due to discrete charge configuration is undefined at the locations of discrete charges. For surface charge distributions, field is discontinuous across the surface.
11. Field due to a charge configuration with total charge zero is not zero, but falls faster than $1/r^2$ at large distances. The electric dipole is the simplest example.
